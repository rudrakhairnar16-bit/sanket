Why this matters

Meet Vaishnavi.

She is deaf. Every water bill, complaint, and certificate in her city gets filed at a municipal counter — across from someone she cannot talk to.

🧏

Day 1

Vaishnavi walks in to pay her water bill. She signs “bill”. The clerk does not understand. She writes a note. She waits.

🧑‍💼

Day 5

The clerk learns “wait”, “form” and “name” in 3-minute lessons on the same desk. The counter starts to speak her language.

📝

Day 14

She signs “thank you” and leaves with her certificate. No note. No wait. That is 30 seconds of service.

The Problem

A counter with no common language.

The moment in Vaishnavi’s story is the same every single day, for millions of deaf citizens — because the people across the counter were never trained, and no tool lives where the counter is.

Tools built for the counter itself

Root cause: ISL lessons exist off the counter — apps, courses, video libraries. Clerks never see them because the need is only visible at the counter.

Consequence: Deaf citizens rely on written notes, family interpreters, or simply leave without service. No accountability, no recourse, no data.

We know the gaps — #1

ISL is not one language.

Signs vary by region, community, and age. A single national recognizer is the wrong target — so we don't build one.

😰 The honest problem

  • → Same word, different handshape in Gujarat vs Maharashtra
  • → School ISL ≠ street ISL ≠ home signs
  • → A model trained on one community fails the next town over

🛠️ How we design for it

  • → ISLRTC-sourced vocabulary + icons + text + TTS — never blocked on one handshape
  • → Regional variant packs, tagged and deployable per municipality
  • → 60-second on-device calibration — the clerk trains their own local variant
  • → Low-confidence sign → human interpreter escalation (roadmap)
We know the gaps — #2

Rural counters are the hardest — and the most important.

The taluka office is where the counter IS the government. It also has the worst power, internet, and training.

⚡ Power & internet

Offline-first PWA: the core flow (34 signs, chat, TTS) runs with no network after first load. MediaPipe runs on-device — no server round-trip for recognition.

🧑‍🏫 No trainer on site

Train-the-trainer: one ISL-trained talati per circle becomes the local node — the ASHA-worker model, applied to accessibility.

📈 Adoption, not installs

Pilot = one taluka office, measured on assisted citizens per month. If the model works where connectivity is worst, it works everywhere.
